People assume bad audio means a bad microphone. Usually it means a good-enough microphone being used at the wrong distance, in the wrong room, at the wrong level. Here is what to change first, in the order that gives you most per minute of effort.

## 1. Get closer

The single biggest improvement available, and it costs nothing.

Sound obeys the inverse square law: halve the distance and roughly quadruple the energy reaching the microphone. What matters is that your voice gets louder while the room does not — you have moved, the walls have not. The ratio of voice to room improves dramatically.

A laptop mic is typically 50–60cm from your face when you sit normally. At 25–30cm it sounds like a different machine.

The practical version: sit forward, or raise the laptop. Do not lean so far in that you are uncomfortable, because you can hear discomfort in a voice.

## 2. Fix the room before the microphone

An empty room with hard walls is the worst possible recording space, and it is what most people have. Sound bounces, arrives late, and smears everything into that hollow "recorded in a bathroom" quality. No microphone fixes it, because the reflections are real.

What actually helps, roughly in order:

- **Soft things.** A sofa, curtains, a rug, a bed. Record in the room with the most fabric in it.
- **Avoid the middle of the room.** Corners and centres are where reflections gather.
- **Do not face a bare wall** from close range. Face into the room, or into something soft.
- **A wardrobe genuinely works.** Standing in front of hanging clothes is a real technique, not a joke — the clothes absorb what would otherwise bounce.

Ten minutes of moving furniture beats a hundred pounds of microphone.

## 3. Set your level properly

Too quiet and you will amplify noise later. Too loud and you clip, which is unfixable — clipped audio has had its peaks cut off and the detail is gone.

Watch the meter while you speak at your normal recording volume. You want it sitting in the middle most of the time, with occasional peaks short of the top. If it hits the ceiling, back off.

The important asymmetry: **quiet can be fixed afterwards, clipping cannot.** When in doubt, record a little quieter than feels right.

## 4. Turn off the processing

Laptops and meeting apps apply noise suppression, echo cancellation and automatic gain control by default. These are tuned for calls, where intelligibility matters more than quality, and they do audible damage to a voice-over — pumping the level, chewing the start of words, and making quiet passages swim.

Check your system sound settings for "noise cancellation" or "voice isolation", and any meeting app running in the background. Quit those apps entirely; some apply processing to the whole system while running.

## 5. Use wired earbuds rather than a Bluetooth headset

This surprises people. A Bluetooth headset switches to a low-bandwidth call profile the moment its microphone is active, and that profile sounds thin and compressed. It is fine for a phone call and wrong for a recording.

Wired earbuds with an inline mic sit close to your mouth and carry full bandwidth. They routinely beat both a Bluetooth headset and the laptop's own microphone.

## 6. Record room tone, and leave handles

Start recording, stay silent for three or four seconds, then begin.

Two reasons. You get a clean piece of silence to trim to, so your recording does not start mid-breath. And you can hear what your background noise actually is — usually a fridge, a fan or traffic you had stopped noticing.

Leave a couple of seconds at the end too, for the same reason.

## 7. Do the boring physical things

The unglamorous list that fixes more takes than any setting:

- **Turn off the fan, the air conditioning and anything with a compressor.** Start the recording and listen back to ten seconds of silence before you commit to a long take.
- **Take off jewellery that clicks**, and watch your sleeves on the desk.
- **Do not type.** A laptop microphone is millimetres from the keyboard and picks up every keystroke as a thump.
- **Silence your phone**, including vibrate — a buzzing phone on a desk is louder to a microphone than it is to you.
- **Drink water.** Mouth noise is the hardest thing to edit out and the easiest to prevent.

## What to do after the take

Trim the ends so the file starts and finishes cleanly. That alone makes a recording sound more deliberate than it did.

If you are sending it, MP3 is the right choice — small, and it plays everywhere. If it is going into an editor, take the WAV, because every re-encode costs a little quality and you may as well not start from a compressed file.

And if a take has a problem you can hear, record it again rather than trying to repair it. Re-recording thirty seconds takes thirty seconds. Repairing thirty seconds of clipped, roomy audio can take an hour and still sound wrong.

## When hardware is actually the answer

If you have done the above and still want better, a USB microphone at close range in a soft room is a genuine step up — mostly because it lets you get properly close without craning at a laptop.

But that is the order. Distance, room, level, processing — then hardware. Doing it the other way round is how people end up with an expensive microphone that still sounds like an empty kitchen.
