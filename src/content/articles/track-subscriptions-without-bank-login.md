Every subscription tracker has the same first screen: connect your bank. It is presented as the convenient option, and it is — for them. It is worth understanding what you are agreeing to before you tap it, and what the alternative actually costs you in effort.

Spoiler: about twenty minutes, once.

## What "connect your bank" actually means

Most consumer finance apps do not have a direct relationship with your bank. They use an aggregator — Plaid, Yodlee, Salt Edge, TrueLayer and similar — sitting between the app and your account.

Depending on the country and the bank, that connection is built one of two ways.

**Open banking.** In the UK, the EU and India, regulated APIs let you grant scoped, revocable, time-limited access without the app ever seeing your password. This is the good version. Access typically expires and has to be renewed, and you can revoke it from your bank.

**Screen scraping.** Where those APIs do not exist, the aggregator may ask for your actual online banking credentials and log in as you. Your password sits with a third party, and the access is neither scoped nor easily revocable.

Either way, the app receives **your entire transaction history**, not only the subscriptions. Every shop, every transfer, every payment, usually going back months or years. Finding eleven recurring charges requires reading everything.

That data is valuable beyond the feature you wanted. Read any such app's privacy policy for what it says about analytics, affiliates and "aggregated insights" — some are scrupulous, some are not, and the difference is not visible from the app store listing.

None of this means the connection is reckless. It means it is a real decision, and it is worth knowing there is another way.

## The three-pass method

This finds nearly everything, and takes about twenty minutes once.

### Pass one: search your email

Almost every subscription sends a receipt, and your inbox is a better record than your memory.

Search for each of these:

- `receipt`
- `your subscription`
- `payment received`
- `renewal`
- `invoice`
- `your plan`
- `thanks for subscribing`
- `auto-renew`

Sort by date and work backwards a year — that catches annual subscriptions, which are the ones people forget and which cost the most.

Write down the service, the amount, and the billing date as you go.

### Pass two: the lists that already exist

Three places keep a subscription list for you, and between them they cover most digital services.

**Google Play** — Play Store → profile → Payments and subscriptions → Subscriptions. Everything billed through an Android app.

**Apple** — Settings → your name → Subscriptions on iPhone. Everything billed through an Apple ID.

**PayPal** — Settings → Payments → Automatic payments. This one catches a surprising number, because plenty of older services bill through PayPal rather than a card.

Anything billed through those three can also be cancelled there, which is worth knowing when a company makes cancelling hard on its own site.

### Pass three: one statement, read properly

Download last month's card or bank statement as a PDF and read every line.

You are looking for the same amount appearing on roughly the same date each month. Two things make this harder than it sounds, so watch for them:

**Merchant names rarely match the brand.** A charge might read `DNH*GODADDY`, `SPOTIFY P4A2B1`, or a payment processor's name with no clue attached. Search the string you see — someone has always asked what it is.

**Annual charges will not appear.** Which is why the email pass goes back a year.

If you have more than one card, do this for each.

## What to do with the list

Sort by annual cost rather than monthly. A ₹199 monthly subscription is ₹2,388 a year, and seeing that number changes decisions that ₹199 does not.

Then put each one in a bucket:

- **Used this week.** Keep.
- **Used this month.** Keep, but check whether a cheaper tier covers what you use.
- **Not used in three months.** Cancel. You can resubscribe, and most services make that easy precisely because they want you back.
- **Did not recognise.** Investigate today rather than later. Sometimes it is a renamed service; sometimes it is a trial that converted; occasionally it is fraud.

Check for overlap while you are there. Several streaming services with the same films, two cloud storage plans, a password manager you pay for plus one bundled free with something else.

## Keeping it from happening again

The list is only useful if it stays current.

**Log new subscriptions when you start them,** including free trials — trials are the single most common route to a forgotten charge, because the decision to pay is made by not acting.

**Set your own reminder before each renewal,** a couple of days ahead. The point is to decide deliberately rather than notice afterwards.

**Use a virtual card for trials** if your bank offers them. Some let you set a limit or a single-use number, which makes an unwanted renewal fail rather than succeed.

**Diarise an annual review.** Once a year, run the three passes again. It takes ten minutes the second time.

## Where an app helps, and where it does not

A tracker earns its place by remembering the dates and doing the arithmetic — showing the true monthly and yearly total, warning before a renewal, splitting shared plans. None of that needs your bank.

What a bank connection genuinely adds is discovery: finding subscriptions you forgot without you searching. That is a real convenience, and the three passes above are the manual version. Whether it is worth your full transaction history is a judgement only you can make — but it should be a judgement, not a default.
