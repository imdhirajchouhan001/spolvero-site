Most timings in a lesson plan are guesses that hardened into habit. Here are durations that tend to work, and — more usefully — the reasoning, so you can adapt them rather than follow them.

## The numbers

| Activity | Typical | Worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do-now / bell work | 3–5 min | Short enough that late arrivals still catch it |
| Think-pair-share | 1 + 2 + 3 min | Think alone, then pairs, then share out |
| Group discussion | 8–12 min | Past 12 minutes most groups drift |
| Independent writing | 10–20 min | Scale with year group |
| Silent reading | 15–30 min | Build up across the term |
| Practical set-up | 5 min | A visible timer cuts this sharply |
| Tidy-up | 3–5 min | Give a one-minute warning |
| Exit ticket | 3–5 min | Keep it short or it eats the lesson |

These are starting points. A Year 7 class and a Year 12 class do not have the same attention span, and neither do two Year 7 classes on different days.

## Why think-pair-share has three numbers

The structure only works if each stage is protected.

**One minute alone** is enough to form a thought and short enough that nobody stalls. Skipping it means the confident students set the direction before anyone else has decided what they think.

**Two minutes in pairs** is enough to compare and disagree. Longer and pairs finish and start talking about something else.

**Three minutes sharing** covers four or five contributions. Trying to hear from everyone turns a five-minute routine into fifteen.

Run as one six-minute block it collapses into unstructured chat. The timing *is* the structure.

## The twelve-minute wall in group work

Group discussion has a fairly reliable arc: a couple of minutes settling, a productive middle, then a decline that starts around the ten to twelve minute mark.

Two options when you need longer. Break it into stages with their own timers — "eight minutes to list, then six to rank". Or change the grouping partway, which resets attention because the audience changed.

What rarely works is one long undifferentiated block with a vague "you've got about twenty minutes".

## Make the time visible

This is the part that matters more than any number above.

A timer on the board changes behaviour in ways that announcing the time does not:

**Students pace themselves.** With a visible countdown they can see they are halfway with half the task left. Without it, pace is your problem rather than theirs.

**The clock becomes the authority.** "Two minutes left" from a screen is information. From a teacher it can feel like pressure, and it invites negotiation. The timer does not negotiate.

**Transitions get faster.** Tidy-up is the clearest case — a five-minute visible countdown reliably beats "right, let's start packing away", because the end point is not in dispute.

**You stop being the clock.** Not having to track time while also teaching frees up attention for the thing you are actually there to do.

## Practical details that decide whether it works

**Make it big enough.** If a student at the back squints, it is decorative. Fullscreen on a smartboard, with digits that read from the furthest desk.

**Use a calm sound, or none.** A siren in a quiet classroom startles people and makes the timer something to dread. A soft chime, or just a colour change, does the job.

**Warn before the end.** A one-minute warning matters most for tidy-up and writing, where stopping abruptly means unfinished work or an unfinished sentence.

**Keep the screen clean.** Anything else on the board becomes the thing students look at. Our [classroom timer](/tools/timers/classroom-timer/) shows nothing but the time in fullscreen — no navigation, no branding, and no ads at all in that view, which is the whole reason it was built that way.

**Do not time everything.** A timer on every activity turns a lesson into a series of countdowns and drains the room. Use it where pace genuinely matters: starters, transitions, bounded tasks, tests.

## When to abandon the timer

Two cases, and recognising them matters more than holding the line.

**The task was harder than you thought.** If most of the class is genuinely mid-thought at zero, adding time is the right call. Say so out loud — "I'm giving you three more minutes, this is harder than I planned" — so it reads as judgement rather than the timer being meaningless.

**Something better is happening.** If a discussion has gone somewhere more interesting than the lesson plan, the plan is what should give way.

The timer is there to protect the pace of the lesson, not to override your reading of the room.
