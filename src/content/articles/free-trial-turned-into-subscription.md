A free trial that required a card is not a trial with a purchase at the end. It is a subscription with a discount at the start. The billing arrangement is already in place; the only thing standing between you and the charge is a date.

Understanding it that way makes the rest of this obvious.

## Cancel early, not on the last day

The most useful fact here, and the one almost nobody acts on:

**On Google Play, cancelling during a free trial does not end the trial.** You keep access until the original end date. Cancel on day two of a fourteen-day trial and you still have twelve days left — you simply do not get charged at the end of them.

So there is no reason at all to wait. Waiting only creates the risk.

And the risk is real, because **the charge often goes through before the date you think it will.** Billing systems commonly process the renewal in the 24 hours before the trial expires. Sitting down to cancel on the morning of the last day is how people end up paying for a month they had decided against.

Apple's subscriptions behave similarly. The details differ, but the principle holds everywhere: cancel as soon as you have decided, not as late as you can.

## Find the exact date

"About two weeks" is not a date, and two weeks is not always what was offered.

**On Google Play.** Play Store → your profile picture → Payments & subscriptions → Subscriptions. Anything in a trial shows the date it converts and the price it converts to. That screen is the source of truth; your memory of the sign-up page is not.

**On the App Store.** Settings → your name → Subscriptions, which lists the renewal date for each one.

**Bought on a website?** It will appear in neither. Go to the confirmation email, which states the trial length, and to the account's billing page, which usually states the conversion date outright.

Write the date down somewhere you will see it. Not the trial length — the date.

## Trial lengths are shorter than they look

Three things routinely shorten the window you think you have.

**The trial starts at sign-up, not at first use.** Subscribing on a Friday and opening the app the following Wednesday spends five of your days on nothing.

**"One month" is not thirty days.** A month-long trial starting on the 31st can end on the 28th. It is a small difference and it lands on exactly the day you were planning to cancel.

**Time zones.** Billing runs on the store's clock, not yours. If your trial ends "today", you may have fewer hours than the screen implies.

Set your reminder two days before. It costs you two days of a trial you were probably not going to convert, and it removes every one of these.

## The patterns worth recognising

None of this is illegal. All of it is designed.

**A card required for a free trial** exists so the conversion needs no action from you. A trial that genuinely does not need payment details will not ask for them.

**A trial that is hard to cancel inside the app** is usually easy to cancel in a browser. If the app has no cancel option, open the same account on the web before concluding there isn't one.

**Annual conversion from a short trial.** Some trials convert to a year, not a month. The subscriptions screen shows which. A seven-day trial that becomes a £90 annual charge is a very different decision from one that becomes £7.99 a month, and the sign-up page rarely makes that distinction loudly.

**"Cancel anytime" is true and not the point.** You can always cancel. The question is whether you will remember to, which is what the whole model is built on.

## If you have already been charged

Move quickly — this is entirely a question of timing.

**Cancel first, then ask for the refund.** Otherwise the next one is coming too.

**Ask the same day if you can.** Google runs a short self-service refund window in the Play Store, after which the decision passes to the developer. Asking within hours works far more often than asking in a fortnight.

**Ask plainly.** "I did not intend to continue past the trial and cancelled as soon as I saw the charge" is a reasonable request, and plenty of developers grant it — an annoyed customer who churns anyway is worth less to them than the refund.

**In the UK and EU**, a subscription taken out entirely online may carry cancellation rights, and some services treat a first charge after a trial more generously than their published policy suggests. It is worth asking rather than assuming.

## Free trials are worth taking

None of this is an argument against them. A trial is genuinely the best way to find out whether something is any good, and most of them are honest.

The problem is only ever the date. Take the trial, decide early, and cancel the moment you decide — which, on Play, costs you nothing at all.
