The timing lights are the part of a Toastmasters meeting that confuses new members most, partly because the signal is deliberately silent and partly because the numbers differ by speech type. Here is the whole system in one place.

## What the three colours mean

They are not a countdown. They mark three specific moments.

**Green — you have reached the minimum.** The speech now qualifies. Everything from here is within the allowed range.

**Amber — one minute left.** Begin landing the speech. This is the signal that matters most, because it is the one that gives a speaker time to act.

**Red — you have reached the maximum.** Finish the sentence and sit down.

The colours are shown by the timekeeper, traditionally with cards and now often with a screen. Nothing is said aloud, because interrupting a speaker to tell them about time would defeat the purpose.

## The times, by speech type

| Speech | Range | Green | Amber | Red |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ice Breaker (Level 1) | 4–6 min | 4:00 | 5:00 | 6:00 |
| Most prepared speeches | 5–7 min | 5:00 | 6:00 | 7:00 |
| Evaluation | 2–3 min | 2:00 | 2:30 | 3:00 |
| Table Topics response | 1–2 min | 1:00 | 1:30 | 2:00 |
| Grammarian, ah-counter, timer reports | 2–3 min | 2:00 | 2:30 | 3:00 |

Two things to notice. For longer speeches, amber sits a full minute before red. For the shorter ones it sits thirty seconds before, because a minute's warning inside a two-minute speech would be most of the speech.

Some Pathways projects specify their own lengths — a few run to 8, 10 or 12 minutes. The project description always states the range, and it overrides the default.

## The 30-second rule

This is where timing stops being guidance and becomes a rule.

**In a contest, a speaker who finishes more than 30 seconds under the minimum or more than 30 seconds over the maximum is disqualified.** A 5–7 minute speech has a hard window of 4:30 to 7:30. Outside it, the speech is not scored, regardless of quality.

The rule exists because contests are competitive and time is one of the few objective constraints available. It is applied strictly, and it is the most common way a good contest speech is lost.

In ordinary club meetings the range is treated as guidance. Going 20 seconds over gets a note in the timer's report, not a penalty.

## Running the timer role

If you have been assigned timekeeper, this is the whole job.

**Before the meeting.** Find out which speeches are happening and their ranges. Check the lights or cards work. If a speaker's project has an unusual length, ask them before the meeting rather than guessing.

**During.** Start on the speaker's first word, or their first deliberate gesture if they open with a pause. Show each colour as it is reached and leave it showing. Keep the clock running past red — you need the final time, and contests need it precisely.

**Afterwards.** Report each speaker's time. Some clubs read every time; others only flag those outside the range. Say the actual times rather than "within time" — speakers use the number to calibrate.

**One detail people miss:** you time the speech, not the walk. The clock starts when they begin speaking, not when they reach the lectern.

## Table Topics is where it gets tricky

Table Topics responses are 1–2 minutes, which is short enough that the lights come in quick succession. Green at one minute, amber at 90 seconds, red at two.

Speakers who have never been timed at that length routinely overrun, because two minutes feels much longer than it is when you are speaking without preparation. It is worth saying so in the introduction.

## Why a screen beats cards

Cards work and clubs used them for decades, but a screen has two practical advantages.

The colour is visible from further away and in worse light, which matters in a room where the speaker is looking into a window.

And the clock keeps running visibly, so when you give your report you are reading a number rather than remembering one.

What a screen must not do is become a distraction. It should show the colour and the time, and nothing else — no notifications, no ads, nothing moving. ## Setting it up

Pick the speech type and the three points are set for you — the presets above are built in. For a club with its own conventions, or a Pathways project with an unusual length, every point can be set by hand.

The clock counts up rather than down, which matches how the role is actually performed: you are recording elapsed time to report, not counting down to an alarm. It keeps running past red for the same reason.

Put the screen where the speaker can see it and the audience cannot be distracted by it — a laptop on the front table, turned towards the lectern, is the arrangement most clubs settle on.
