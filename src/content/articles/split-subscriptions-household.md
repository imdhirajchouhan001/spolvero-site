Shared subscriptions go wrong in a predictable order. Somebody pays for everything on one card, everyone agrees to settle up, nobody writes down what the actual total is, and four months later there is a conversation about whether the music one was ever included.

The fix is not an app or a spreadsheet. It is getting two things straight first.

## Convert everything to the same period

This is where most shared subscription arithmetic falls apart, and it takes thirty seconds to fix.

A £120 annual plan is **£10 a month**. A £12.99 monthly plan is **£155.88 a year**. Until every subscription is expressed in the same period, you cannot compare them, split them, or know what the household actually spends.

Annual plans also distort a split in a way people feel unfairly. One person pays £120 in March and is then owed in monthly instalments by people who may not be living there in September. Agree what happens to that before it becomes an argument:

- **Settle it as a monthly share.** Simplest, and matches how everyone experiences the service.
- **Settle it up front.** Fine between people who stay put, awkward when someone moves out in month seven.
- **Pro-rate on leaving.** Fairest and the most work. Only worth it for expensive annual plans.

Pick one and apply it to every annual subscription rather than deciding case by case.

## Even splits are usually the wrong split

An even split is easy and often unfair, which is why it produces resentment slowly rather than immediately.

**Split evenly** where everyone genuinely uses it about the same: broadband, a shared streaming service everyone watches, a cloud storage plan holding everyone's photos.

**Split by user** where they do not. A family plan with six seats and three people using it costs three shares, not six. The unused seats are not a cost anyone has to carry.

**Do not split at all** where one person wanted it. A gym app, a design tool, a game pass. Somebody agreeing to chip in for something they never open is the arrangement most likely to quietly stop being paid.

The useful question is not "what does this cost" but "who would still buy this if they were paying alone". Anyone who would not should probably not be paying a share.

## Check what sharing is actually allowed

Worth doing before you build a household system on top of an assumption.

**Family plans usually mean one address.** Most services define a family as a household, not a friendship group, and several verify it — some by requiring periodic connection from a home network, some by checking the address on the account. This is enforced more than it used to be, and losing access mid-month is a worse outcome than paying for a second plan.

**Sharing one personal account generally breaks the terms.** It also tends to work badly in practice: shared watch histories, recommendations that make no sense, and one person getting logged out when another signs in on a new device.

**Some plans are built for exactly this.** Duo tiers for two people at one address, family tiers for up to six, and education or regional pricing where you qualify. Where a legitimate shared plan exists it is almost always cheaper than the workaround.

**Read what happens at renewal.** A shared plan where one person holds the card renews on their card. If that person leaves, the plan does not automatically move.

## Put one person in charge of the list, not the money

A subtle point that solves most of the recurring friction.

The person whose card is used does not have to be the person who keeps the record — and often should not be, because they are the one with an interest in the total. What matters is that **one shared list exists** which everyone can see, showing what is paid, by whom, how often, and what each person owes.

Once that list is visible, the monthly conversation stops being a negotiation. Nobody is remembering, nobody is reconstructing it from a statement, and a subscription nobody can justify becomes obvious without anyone having to raise it.

## Review it twice a year, together

Household subscriptions accumulate. Somebody signs up for a documentary, somebody else for a free trial, and neither is ever revisited.

Two things worth doing every six months:

**Name every one out loud.** If nobody in the room can say what a service is for, that is the answer.

**Check the seats.** Family plans hold seats for people who have moved out, changed service, or stopped watching. Reclaiming them changes nothing about the bill but stops you paying for a tier you no longer need.
